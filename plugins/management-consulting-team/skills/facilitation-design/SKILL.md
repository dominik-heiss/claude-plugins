# Facilitation Design — Principles

Every workshop needs a clear decision output. If there's no decision, it's a presentation — call it one.

## The Core Principle

**A workshop is a decision-making machine.** You bring the right people together, give them the right information, run them through a structured process, and produce a decision (or a prioritized set of options). Everything in the design serves this: participant selection, pre-work, agenda, exercises, and synthesis.

Participant pre-work improves quality 3x. Never skip it. A 2-hour workshop with 30 minutes of pre-work outperforms a 4-hour workshop with no preparation.

## Workshop Design

**Design backwards from the output:**
1. What decision or output does this session produce?
2. What inputs do participants need to make that decision?
3. What process moves them from inputs to output?
4. Who needs to be in the room for this process to produce a legitimate result?

**Workshop types and their outputs:**

| Type | Output | Duration | Participants |
|------|--------|----------|-------------|
| Alignment | Agreed priorities or direction | 2-3 hours | Decision-makers (6-10) |
| Working session | Draft deliverable or analysis | 3-4 hours | Working team (4-8) |
| Ideation | Prioritized list of options | 2-3 hours | Cross-functional group (8-15) |
| Decision | Go/no-go or selection among options | 1-2 hours | Decision-makers (3-6) |
| Review | Validated deliverable with feedback incorporated | 1-2 hours | Reviewers + authors (4-8) |

**Participant count:** 6-10 is the productive range for discussion-based sessions. Below 5 lacks diversity of perspective. Above 12 forces passive participation — large groups require breakout structures.

## Agenda Structuring

**The 20-60-20 rule:**
- **20% — Context setting:** Align on the purpose, share the inputs, establish the ground rules. This is NOT a 45-minute presentation — it's the minimum context needed for productive work.
- **60% — Working time:** The exercises, discussions, and decision-making that produce the output. This is the workshop.
- **20% — Synthesis and next steps:** Capture decisions, identify open items, assign actions. Synthesize during the session, not after.

**Every agenda block has:**
- A question it answers (not a topic — a question)
- A method (how participants will work on it)
- A time box (strictly enforced)
- An output (what this block produces)

**Time boxing is non-negotiable.** Parkinson's law applies to workshops more than anywhere. A discussion that "needs 45 minutes" will take 90 if allowed. Set the time, enforce it, park overflow items.

## Exercise Selection

Choose exercises based on what you need participants to do:

**Divergent exercises (generate options):**
- **Silent brainstorming:** Each participant writes ideas individually before sharing. Prevents anchoring on the first speaker's ideas. 5-7 minutes silent, then share and cluster.
- **Round-robin:** Each participant contributes one idea in turn. Ensures equal contribution from all participants regardless of seniority.
- **Scenario brainstorming:** "What if [extreme scenario]? What would we do?" Breaks mental models and surfaces non-obvious options.

**Convergent exercises (narrow and prioritize):**
- **Dot voting:** Each participant gets 3-5 votes to allocate across options. Fast, democratic, visual. Works best with 8-20 options to narrow to 3-5.
- **Force-ranking:** Participants rank all options from most to least preferred. Produces a clear priority list. More rigorous than dot voting but takes longer.
- **2x2 prioritization:** Plot options on impact vs. feasibility (or any two relevant dimensions). Creates natural quadrants: quick wins, strategic bets, fill-ins, deprioritize.
- **Effort-impact matrix:** Variant of 2x2 specifically for initiative prioritization. Useful when participants have different views on what's hard vs. what's impactful.

**Evaluative exercises (stress-test and challenge):**
- **Pre-mortem:** "It's 12 months from now and this initiative has failed. Why?" Forces participants to identify risks they'd otherwise suppress in the enthusiasm of planning.
- **Red team / blue team:** Split participants into advocates and challengers. Surfaces weaknesses in the argument more effectively than open discussion.
- **Assumption surfacing:** List all assumptions underlying the plan. Rate each on confidence level. Focus discussion on low-confidence, high-impact assumptions.

**Synthesis exercises (align and commit):**
- **"One thing" round:** Each participant states the one thing they need to be true for this to succeed. Reveals alignment gaps.
- **Decision matrix:** Score options against weighted criteria. Produces a defensible, documented decision.
- **Headline test:** "What's the headline of our decision today?" Forces the group to articulate the output in one sentence.

## Stakeholder Interview Guides

**Design interviews to test hypotheses, not collect opinions.**

**Interview structure (45-60 minutes):**
1. **Warm-up (5 min):** Role, tenure, scope of responsibility. Establishes context and builds rapport.
2. **Open-ended exploration (15 min):** "What are the biggest challenges facing [topic]?" Let them talk. Listen for themes that confirm or challenge your hypotheses.
3. **Hypothesis probing (20 min):** "We're hearing that [hypothesis]. Does that match your experience?" "What would you point to as evidence for or against?"
4. **Specific data questions (10 min):** "Do you know the current [metric]?" "Who would have data on [topic]?"
5. **Close (5 min):** "What haven't we asked about that we should?" "Who else should we speak with?"

**Interview discipline:**
- Never lead the witness. "Don't you think the market is growing?" is a leading question. "How do you see the market evolving?" is not.
- Take verbatim quotes on key points. Paraphrasing loses nuance.
- Interview in pairs when possible: one leads, one takes notes.
- Write up findings within 24 hours. Memory decays fast.

## Synthesizing Qualitative Input

**Synthesis happens during the session, not after.** If you leave a 3-hour workshop with raw notes and no synthesized output, you've lost most of the value.

**Real-time synthesis techniques:**
- **Affinity clustering:** Group similar ideas physically (sticky notes, digital board). Name the clusters. The cluster names become your themes.
- **Polling for agreement:** After discussion, quick show of hands or scale vote: "On a 1-5 scale, how aligned are you with this direction?" Surfaces silent disagreement.
- **Summary playback:** Every 30-45 minutes, the facilitator summarizes: "Here's what I'm hearing: [3 points]. Is that right?" Corrects misunderstandings in real time.

**Post-session synthesis:**
- Consolidate within 4 hours while memory is fresh
- Structure findings as: theme, evidence (specific quotes/data points), implication for hypothesis
- Distinguish between consensus findings (most participants agreed) and split findings (divergent views). Both are valuable — splits are often more informative.

## Common Traps

**Death by presentation.** The first 60 minutes is slides. Participants are passive. The "discussion" is actually Q&A. Counter: cap context-setting at 20% of total time. Send pre-read materials.

**The loudest voice wins.** Senior participants dominate. Junior participants stay silent. Counter: use silent writing before discussion, structured turn-taking, or anonymous voting.

**No decisions, just discussion.** The session ends with "good discussion, let's follow up." Nothing was decided. Counter: define the decision output at the start. Check at the halfway point: "Are we on track to reach a decision by the end?"

**Synthesizing after the session.** The facilitator goes away and writes up "what was decided." Participants later disagree with the write-up because the synthesis reflects the facilitator's interpretation, not the group's conclusion. Counter: synthesize and confirm in the room.

**Too many exercises.** Running 6 exercises in 2 hours leaves no time for depth. Each exercise gets 15 minutes and produces shallow output. Counter: 2-3 exercises maximum per session, with adequate time for each.

**Skipping pre-work.** "People are busy, they won't do pre-work." True — unless the pre-work is short (15-30 minutes), clearly connected to the session output, and personally relevant. Design pre-work that participants want to do because it asks for their perspective.
