# Executive Storylining — Principles

The answer first. Always.

## The Core Principle

**Pyramid principle (Barbara Minto):** Give the answer first, then the supporting arguments, then the evidence. Never the reverse.

Executives don't want to follow your reasoning process. They want to know what you recommend, then decide whether to scrutinize your reasoning. Respecting this is the foundation of executive communication.

## The Pyramid Structure

```
GOVERNING THOUGHT (1 sentence)
"Company X should enter Market Y via acquisition of Target Z."
         │
         ├── KEY LINE ITEM 1 (reason 1)
         │   "The market is large and growing"
         │   ├── Support 1a: Market size EUR 45B, 8% CAGR (Slide 3)
         │   └── Support 1b: Regulatory tailwinds accelerate through 2030 (Slide 4)
         │
         ├── KEY LINE ITEM 2 (reason 2)
         │   "Organic entry would take 5+ years — acquisition compresses timeline to 18 months"
         │   ├── Support 2a: Time-to-scale analysis (Slide 5)
         │   └── Support 2b: Target Z has customer relationships X cannot build from scratch (Slide 6)
         │
         └── KEY LINE ITEM 3 (reason 3)
             "Acquisition economics are attractive under base-case assumptions"
             ├── Support 3a: NPV positive at EUR 45M; IRR of 18% (Slide 7)
             └── Support 3b: Synergy case is conservative and achievable (Slide 8)
```

**Governing thought:** A single sentence that answers the core question. It should be the one thing someone remembers if they forget everything else.

**Key line items:** The 3-5 reasons why the governing thought is true. These are the sections of the deck.

**Supporting arguments:** Evidence, analysis, data that proves each key line item. These are the slides.

## The "So What?" Test

Every slide, every section, every finding must answer: "So what? What does this mean for the decision?"

Apply this test: Cover the takeaway (action title or conclusion) and ask — "If I removed this takeaway, would anything be lost?" If not, the takeaway wasn't specific enough.

Examples:
- ❌ "The market grew from EUR 20B to EUR 45B over 5 years." — this is an observation, not a "so what"
- ✅ "Market doubled in 5 years — scale is now sufficient to justify entry." — this is a "so what"

## Storyline Before Slides

**Develop the storyline in writing first.** Only start building slides when the argument is complete and coherent.

A common mistake: building slides in parallel with the analysis. This embeds structural decisions prematurely, makes it harder to restructure when conclusions change, and produces decks that feel assembled rather than argued.

**Storyline = the argument.** Slides = its visual expression.

If you can't write the storyline in words, you don't have a clear argument yet.

## Action Titles

Every slide's title must deliver the key message — not describe the topic.

| What it describes | What it says — the action title |
|------------------|---------------------------------|
| Market Overview | European EV charging market reached EUR 45B in 2024, growing at 8% CAGR |
| Competitive Landscape | Three incumbents dominate but none has scale advantage — entry window is open |
| Financial Analysis | Acquisition NPV of EUR 45M exceeds organic growth option by EUR 20M at base case |
| Risk Assessment | Two risks are material; both can be mitigated with standard deal structuring |
| Recommendation | Acquire Target Z at EUR 120-150M to accelerate entry by 3+ years |

**Test:** Can a reader understand the key message from the title alone, without reading the slide? If yes, it's an action title.

## Narrative Opening Patterns

**Situation-Complication-Resolution (SCR):**
- Situation: "Company X is the market leader in [current market], growing steadily."
- Complication: "However, [disruption/trend] is creating pressure on core margins and the window for [strategic option] is closing."
- Resolution: "To protect long-term position, we recommend [action]."

Use SCR when the audience needs context to understand why the recommendation matters.

**Direct (Answer First):**
- Lead with the recommendation
- Follow with the 3 reasons why
- Then the evidence

Use when the audience already understands the problem and wants the answer.

**Action-Oriented:**
- Start with the decision required
- State the recommended path
- Justify it

Use for board presentations or when time is the constraint.

## C-Level Communication Rules

- **Brevity:** Every slide that can be removed should be removed.
- **Clarity:** If a CFO cannot understand a slide in 30 seconds, it's too complex.
- **Action orientation:** Every conclusion should imply an action or decision.
- **Risk acknowledgment:** Executives distrust recommendations that don't acknowledge downside risks. Name the risks and how they're managed.
- **Confidence calibration:** Don't hedge everything; don't overstate certainty. "Our analysis strongly suggests X" is better than "the data might indicate X" or "we are certain X."
