# Engagement Governance — Principles

Scope creep is the #1 engagement risk. Everything else is manageable.

## The Core Principle

**An engagement exists to answer a question and deliver an artifact. Everything you do either moves toward that answer or is scope creep.** The Engagement Manager's primary governance job is maintaining this discipline — not through bureaucracy, but through relentless clarity about what's in scope, what's out, and what's changed.

Every week, ask: "If we delivered today, what's missing?" The answer is your priority list. Everything else waits.

## Scope Management

**The scope document is a contract.** It defines:
- The core question the engagement answers
- The deliverables the client receives
- The analytical approach (what you will and won't do)
- The timeline and key milestones
- What the client provides (data, access, decisions)
- What is explicitly out of scope

**Scope change protocol:**
1. Someone requests something outside the current scope (client, team, partner)
2. EM assesses: Is this a clarification of existing scope or genuinely new?
3. If new: document the request, estimate the impact on timeline and resources
4. Present to the Principal/Partner: "This is new scope. Here's what it costs in time and resources. My recommendation: [include/exclude/defer to Phase 2]."
5. Decision is documented. If included, the scope document is updated.

**Never silently absorb scope additions.** Even if the addition is small, documenting it maintains discipline and creates a record if the engagement expands materially.

**The "parking lot":** Maintain a running list of out-of-scope items that could be valuable. This shows the client you heard their concerns while protecting the current engagement's focus. These become inputs for follow-on engagement scoping.

## Phase Gate Discipline

**A phase gate is a decision point, not a status update.** At each gate, the Principal/Partner makes one of three decisions:
1. **Proceed** — work meets quality standards, move to the next phase
2. **Rework** — specific items need revision before proceeding
3. **Pivot** — findings require a change in direction, scope, or approach

**Phase gate requirements (non-negotiable):**
- All phase deliverables are complete (not "almost done" — done)
- Review cascade is finished (QA + Partner minimum)
- Critical review findings are addressed (not just acknowledged — fixed)
- Hypothesis tree is updated to reflect current evidence
- Next phase plan is drafted (not detailed — directional)

**Do not skip phase gates under time pressure.** Skipping a gate to "save time" costs more time downstream when quality problems compound. A 2-hour gate review can prevent 20 hours of rework.

## Deliverable Quality Standards

Every deliverable passes through three filters:

**1. The "So What?" test:** Remove the key takeaway. Does the rest of the deliverable still make sense without it? If yes, the takeaway wasn't sharp enough — it was a summary, not an insight.

**2. The evidence test:** Every claim in the deliverable traces to a source. No orphan assertions. No "it is widely known that." If you can't cite it, you can't claim it.

**3. The action test:** Does the deliverable tell the reader what to do or decide? Analysis without a recommendation is an academic exercise. Every deliverable should answer: "Based on this analysis, you should..."

**Quality checklist for written deliverables:**
- [ ] Core question stated in the first paragraph
- [ ] Key takeaway stated explicitly (not left for the reader to infer)
- [ ] Every quantitative claim has a source reference
- [ ] MECE structure in any enumeration or categorization
- [ ] Assumptions stated and flagged by confidence level
- [ ] Limitations acknowledged
- [ ] Recommendations are specific and actionable

## Review Cascade

The review cascade exists because no single person catches all errors, and different reviewers catch different types of issues.

**Sequence (never skip or reorder):**

| Step | Reviewer | Focus |
|------|----------|-------|
| 1 | QA Reviewer | Logic, MECE, source accuracy, internal consistency, numerical checks |
| 2 | Revision | Author addresses QA findings |
| 3 | Partner Advisor | Strategic sharpness, storyline quality, "so what?" strength |
| 4 | Revision | Author addresses Partner findings |
| 5 | Client Lens (if applicable) | Feasibility, political implications, buy-in probability |
| 6 | Final revision | Address critical Client Lens findings |
| 7 | Partner sign-off | "Ready for client" |

**Review finding classification:**
- **Critical:** Factual error, logical flaw, or missing analysis that would undermine credibility. Must fix before proceeding.
- **Major:** Significant gap in argumentation, weak evidence, or structural issue. Should fix.
- **Minor:** Wording, formatting, presentation issues. Fix if time permits.

**The author never reviews their own work.** This is absolute. Self-review catches typos, not logical gaps.

## Escalation Protocols

**When to escalate (EM to Partner):**
- Client changes scope or expectations materially
- Key data or access is not provided as agreed
- A finding fundamentally changes the engagement direction
- Team capacity issue threatens a deliverable deadline
- Quality of a deliverable is below standard after revision

**When to escalate (team to EM):**
- Blocked by missing data or access for more than one work cycle
- Finding contradicts a confirmed hypothesis
- Task scope is unclear or conflicts with another assignment
- Quality concern about another team member's output

**Escalation format:** State the issue, its impact, and your recommended resolution. Never escalate without a recommendation.

## Client Communication

**Cadence and content:**
- **Kick-off:** Scope, approach, team, timeline, asks of the client
- **Status updates:** Weekly or bi-weekly — progress, findings to date, upcoming asks
- **Steering committees:** At phase gates — findings, recommendations, decisions needed
- **Ad hoc:** When a finding changes direction or a blocker emerges

**Rules for client communication:**
1. Never surprise the client at a SteerCo. Significant findings are previewed in advance.
2. Never present uncertain findings as conclusions. Flag confidence levels.
3. Always arrive with a recommendation, not just analysis. The client hired you for judgment.
4. Keep status updates brief. The client cares about findings and decisions, not about your process.

## Issue and Risk Tracking

**Distinguish clearly:**
- **Issue:** A problem that exists now and requires resolution
- **Risk:** A potential problem that may or may not materialize

**For each issue:**
- Description, owner, impact, resolution plan, target resolution date

**For each risk:**
- Description, likelihood (H/M/L), impact (H/M/L), mitigation plan, trigger indicator

**Review issues and risks at every checkpoint.** Stale issue logs are worse than no issue log — they create false confidence.

## Common Traps

**Governance as theater.** Status meetings where everyone says "on track" and no decisions are made. Counter: every governance meeting must produce at least one decision or action item. If there's nothing to decide, cancel the meeting.

**Over-documenting, under-deciding.** Spending more time on tracking templates than on actual analysis. Governance should be the lightest structure that maintains quality and visibility.

**Avoiding difficult conversations.** A deliverable isn't good enough but no one says it. A scope addition is clearly scope creep but no one pushes back. The EM's job includes having uncomfortable conversations early, before they become crises.

**Conflating activity with progress.** "We've completed 14 research briefs" is activity. "We've confirmed H1 and rejected H2, which narrows the recommendation to two options" is progress. Report on the latter.

**Letting the review cascade become a bottleneck.** Reviews must have time-boxed turnaround. QA review: 1 cycle. Partner review: 1 cycle. If reviews consistently take longer, the governance model needs adjustment.
