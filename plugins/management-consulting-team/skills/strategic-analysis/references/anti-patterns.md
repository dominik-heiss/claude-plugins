# Strategic Analysis Anti-Patterns — Reference

When frameworks create more noise than signal. When to stop and think differently.

---

## Framework Anti-Patterns

### The Template Trap
**What:** Filling in a framework template produces a deliverable that looks complete but contains no real insight.
**Example:** A SWOT with 5 bullets in each quadrant. The bullets describe facts; none says "so what."
**Fix:** For every element in any framework, ask: "So what does this mean for the recommendation?" If you can't answer, the element isn't sharp enough.

### The Completeness Illusion
**What:** A fully completed framework gives a false sense that the analysis is done.
**Example:** Porter Five Forces with all five factors analyzed — but none of the factors actually affects the specific investment decision.
**Fix:** Before running any framework, define in advance which 2-3 factors will most influence the answer. Analyze those deeply. The rest gets minimal treatment.

### Framework Mismatch
**What:** Using a framework designed for one question to answer a different one.
**Example:** Using Ansoff Matrix (which answers "how to grow") to explain "why we're losing market share."
**Fix:** See `framework-selector.md`. Always match the framework to the decision, not to what the team is comfortable with.

### The 2x2 Reflex
**What:** Defaulting to a 2x2 matrix whenever prioritization or evaluation is needed. Most 2x2s are variations of "effort vs. impact" and produce the same answer regardless of context.
**Fix:** Ask whether the two axes are actually the right dimensions for this decision. Often, a simple ranked list with explicit criteria is more defensible.

## When to Drop the Framework Entirely

### Genuinely Novel Situations
When the industry or business model is truly new, most frameworks were built for established industries. Force-fitting them produces the wrong structure.
**Alternative:** Define the key value drivers from first principles. Ask: "What determines whether this business model creates value?" Build the analysis from there.

### When the Client Already Knows
If the client has been in the industry for 20 years and has seen 10 Porter analyses of it, another one adds no value. They know the competitive dynamics.
**Alternative:** Go deeper on the specific decision, not broader on industry context. Use the client's tacit knowledge as a data source.

### When Speed Is Critical
A full framework analysis takes time. If the question is urgent and the answer is 80% clear from a quick scan, don't spend two days doing a complete Five Forces.
**Alternative:** Rapid hypothesis test: identify the 1-2 factors that could change the answer, assess them quickly, confirm or reject the working assumption.

## Rethinking Standard Frameworks

These aren't anti-patterns — they're "use with caution" situations:

**SWOT:** Only useful for initial brainstorming or board communication (they expect it). Always follow with a clear "strategic implications" section — don't leave the SWOT as the deliverable.

**Benchmarking:** Benchmarking can anchor strategy to the wrong reference class. "Best-in-class" assumes current competitors are the right benchmark. In disrupted industries, the right benchmark might be outside the industry entirely.

**BCG Matrix:** Works for portfolio strategy in diversified companies. In single-business firms, the 2x2 is often forced. Better to analyze competitive position and market attractiveness separately with more nuance.

**PESTLE:** Lists macro forces but doesn't tell you which ones matter for this business. Always follow with: "Of these 15 factors, which 2-3 create strategic risk or opportunity for Company X in the next 3-5 years?"
