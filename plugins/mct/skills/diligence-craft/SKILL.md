# Diligence Craft — Principles

Due diligence is the art of finding what someone doesn't want you to find — or what they themselves don't know.

## The Core Principle

**Separate facts from management assertions.** Every claim in a management presentation, CIM, or data room document is an assertion until independently verified. The diligence team's job is to determine which assertions are facts, which are reasonable estimates, and which are optimistic projections dressed up as plans.

The three questions that drive every diligence:
1. **Is the business what they say it is?** (Commercial diligence)
2. **Are the numbers what they say they are?** (Financial diligence)
3. **What could go wrong?** (Risk assessment)

## Red Flag Identification

Train yourself to notice these patterns — each is a signal to dig deeper:

**Revenue red flags:**
- Customer concentration >20% in a single customer
- Revenue acceleration in the sell-side period (hockey stick in the last 12 months)
- Large one-off contracts recognized as recurring revenue
- Channel stuffing indicators: inventory at distributors growing faster than sell-through
- Revenue recognized on percentage-of-completion with aggressive milestone definitions

**Margin red flags:**
- EBITDA adjustments exceeding 20% of reported EBITDA
- "Run-rate" margins that have never actually been achieved for a full quarter
- Cost deferrals into capex that arguably should be opex
- Related-party transactions at non-market terms inflating margins

**Growth red flags:**
- Organic growth claims that don't reconcile when acquisitions are stripped out
- Market growth assumptions higher than any third-party estimate
- Market share gains in every segment simultaneously (statistically implausible)
- Pipeline or backlog that hasn't historically converted at claimed rates

**Operational red flags:**
- Key person dependencies with no succession plan
- Deferred maintenance or capex holidays in the last 2-3 years
- Customer churn data not provided or "not tracked"
- Employee turnover materially above industry norms

## Information Request Lists

Structure requests by workstream, not by data type:

1. **Corporate structure** — legal entities, org chart, governance, regulatory licenses
2. **Commercial** — revenue by customer/product/geo, pipeline, contracts, pricing history, churn
3. **Financial** — audited statements (3 years), management accounts (monthly, 2 years), bridge from reported to adjusted EBITDA, working capital detail, capex breakdown
4. **Operations** — capacity utilization, key supplier contracts, technology stack, IP portfolio
5. **People** — headcount evolution, key management bios and tenure, compensation structure, open positions
6. **Legal/regulatory** — pending litigation, compliance history, material contracts, change of control clauses

**Prioritize ruthlessly.** Request everything but triage what you analyze first: financials and customer data before corporate structure and legal.

## Management Presentation Analysis

The management presentation is a selling document. Read it as one.

**What to look for:**
- What is NOT in the presentation? Missing topics are often more revealing than present ones.
- Where do the numbers in the presentation differ from the data room? Reconcile every key figure.
- What assumptions underlie the management case / business plan? Are they stated or hidden?
- How does management explain past performance shortfalls? Do they take ownership or externalize?

**Expert session preparation:** Come with specific hypotheses to test, not open-ended questions. "We believe customer churn is 15%, not the 8% stated — can you walk us through the methodology?" is 10x more productive than "Tell us about customer retention."

## Data Room Review Priorities

**Day 1 priorities** (what to pull and analyze immediately):
1. Audited financial statements and auditor notes (especially qualifications)
2. Monthly management accounts for trailing 24 months
3. Revenue by top 20 customers for 3 years
4. Customer contracts (top 10 by revenue)
5. EBITDA bridge from reported to adjusted

**Week 1 priorities:**
1. Full customer cohort analysis (if available)
2. Working capital detail and seasonality patterns
3. Capex breakdown: maintenance vs. growth
4. Employee data: headcount, tenure, turnover
5. Key supplier and partnership agreements

## Synergy Sizing

**Revenue synergies:** High uncertainty, low credibility. Never anchor a deal thesis on revenue synergies alone. Categories: cross-sell, pricing power, geographic expansion, new channel access. Apply a realization haircut of 30-50% to any bottom-up revenue synergy estimate.

**Cost synergies:** Higher certainty, more credible. Categories: headcount (overlap in corporate functions), procurement (combined purchasing power), facilities (consolidation), technology (system rationalization). Apply a realization haircut of 10-20%.

**For each synergy line item:**
- Source: where does the saving or revenue come from?
- Magnitude: how much, with a range
- Timing: when does it fully realize? (Most synergies take 2-3 years)
- Cost to achieve: what one-time costs are required to capture this synergy?
- Risk: what could prevent realization?

**Net synergies = gross synergies - cost to achieve - dis-synergies.** Always present the net number. Customer losses from integration disruption, key talent departures, and cultural friction are real dis-synergies.

## Risk Assessment

Structure risks in a 2x2: likelihood x impact. Focus attention on the upper-right quadrant (high likelihood, high impact).

For each material risk:
- **Description** — what could happen
- **Trigger** — what would cause it
- **Impact** — quantified where possible (EUR impact on EBITDA, deal value)
- **Mitigation** — what protections exist (contractual, operational, structural)
- **Diligence action** — what additional analysis would reduce uncertainty

**Always assess "what could go wrong" as a standalone section.** Do not bury risks inside other analyses. The acquirer needs a clear-eyed view of downside scenarios.

## Common Traps

**Falling in love with the deal.** The diligence team's job is to find problems, not to confirm the investment thesis. Maintain skepticism throughout.

**Anchoring on management's numbers.** Build your own base case independently before comparing to management's plan. Differences between the two are the most productive areas to investigate.

**Underweighting qualitative findings.** Culture, management quality, and customer relationships are harder to quantify but often determine post-deal success. Don't dismiss them because they don't fit in a spreadsheet.

**Scope creep in diligence.** Define the critical questions at the start. Not every data room document needs analysis. Focus on what changes the deal thesis or valuation.

**Confusing "no evidence of problems" with "evidence of no problems."** An empty litigation file might mean no litigation — or it might mean the documents weren't uploaded.
